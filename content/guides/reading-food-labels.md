## The 60-second method

1. **Serving size.** All figures are for one serving. If you eat the packet and it holds two, double everything.
2. **Added sugars, sodium, saturated fat.** Lower is better.
3. **Fibre and protein.** Higher is better.
4. **Ingredients.** Listed by weight, largest first.

## The 5 and 20 rule

On U.S. labels, the % Daily Value shows how much of a day's limit or target one serving provides.

- **5% or less** is low.
- **20% or more** is high.

So 25% of the daily value for sodium in one serving is a lot; 25% for fibre is excellent.

## Daily reference amounts

| Nutrient | Daily value or limit |
| --- | --- |
| Added sugars | 50 g (lower is better) |
| Sodium | 2,300 mg, about one teaspoon of salt |
| Saturated fat | 20 g |
| Fibre | 28 g |

The WHO's salt target is tighter: under 5 g of salt (2,000 mg sodium) a day. About three-quarters of the salt people eat is already in packaged and restaurant food.

## Traffic lights

UK front-of-pack labels colour-code fat, saturates, sugars and salt per 100 g. More greens and ambers than reds is the quick test. Comparing per 100 g is the fairest way to choose between two products.

## Ingredient list clues

A long list is not automatically bad, but these suggest a highly processed product:

- Sugar, refined flour or oil among the first three ingredients.
- Several kinds of sugar or syrup.
- Ingredients you would not find in a kitchen: emulsifiers, flavour enhancers, colours, modified starches.

Diets high in such foods are linked with weight gain and poorer health, and current guidelines advise limiting them. Foods with one ingredient, such as oats, beans, eggs, plain yoghurt and frozen vegetables, need no decoding.

## Claims on the front

"Natural", "multigrain", "made with real fruit" and "light" have loose or no legal meaning. "Low fat" products often add sugar. Turn the packet over; the panel on the back is the regulated part.
