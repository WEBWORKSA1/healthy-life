Stress, and what slow breathing does about it

How the stress response works, five ways to bring it down, and when stress needs more than self-help.

By the Healthy.Life editorial team. 5 minute read. Last reviewed 19 November 2026.

The short version

  • Short bursts of stress are normal; trouble starts when you never come down.
  • Slow breathing with a longer out-breath is the fastest lever you control.
  • Movement, sleep, time outdoors and people are the long-term foundation.

What stress is

When your brain reads a threat, it releases adrenaline and cortisol. Heart rate and blood pressure rise, breathing quickens, muscles tense and digestion pauses. It is a superb system for short emergencies.

Modern stress is rarely short. Deadlines, money, caring duties and a constant feed of news keep the system partly switched on. Over months that is linked with poor sleep, raised blood pressure, more illness, low mood and unhelpful coping such as overeating, drinking and smoking.

The fastest lever: your breath

You cannot will your heart to slow, but you can slow your breathing, and the heart follows. Breathing at about six breaths a minute, with the out-breath longer than the in-breath, stimulates the vagus nerve and shifts the body towards its rest state.

Try it now: breathe in through your nose for 4, out for 6. Repeat ten times. That is under two minutes.

Reviews of controlled trials find that regular slow-breathing practice produces small to moderate reductions in stress and anxiety. The breathing timer paces four patterns for you.

Four more that work

  • Move. A brisk 10-minute walk burns off stress hormones and improves mood for hours.
  • Sleep. Short sleep makes the same stressor feel bigger the next day.
  • Go outside. Time in green space lowers heart rate and reported stress within about 20 minutes.
  • Talk. Telling someone what is going on is one of the most effective stress reducers known.

Dealing with the cause

Calming your body buys space to think. Then sort what is stressing you into two lists: things you can act on, and things you cannot. For the first, write the smallest next step. For the second, practise letting the thought pass rather than arguing with it; mindfulness and writing things down both help.

Habits that backfire

Alcohol, late-night scrolling and skipping meals or exercise feel like relief and add to the load. Notice which one you reach for.

When it is more than stress

See a doctor if you feel anxious or low most days for two weeks or more, if you have panic attacks, if you cannot function at work or home, or if you use alcohol or drugs to cope. If you have thoughts of harming yourself, contact a crisis line or emergency service in your country straight away. Help is available and it works.

Questions people ask

Does deep breathing actually reduce stress?

Yes, modestly and reliably. Slow, paced breathing lowers heart rate and blood pressure in the moment and reduces reported stress with regular practice.

How do I know if I am too stressed?

Common signs are poor sleep, irritability, headaches or tense muscles, trouble concentrating, and leaning harder on food, alcohol or screens.
