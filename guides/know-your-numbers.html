Know your numbers: the six that matter

Blood pressure, cholesterol, blood sugar, waist, resting heart rate and weight: what healthy looks like and how to find yours.

By the Healthy.Life editorial team. 5 minute read. Last reviewed 19 November 2026.

The short version

  • Six numbers describe most of your heart and metabolic risk.
  • Three you can measure at home today; three need a blood test.
  • Trends over time tell you more than any single reading.

The six

NumberHealthy range for most adultsHow to get it
Blood pressureUnder 120/80 mm HgHome monitor or pharmacy
LDL cholesterolUnder 100 mg/dL (2.6 mmol/L)Blood test
Blood sugar (A1C)Under 5.7%Blood test
Waist-to-height ratioUnder 0.5Tape measure
Resting heart rate60 to 100 beats a minuteFingers and a clock
Body mass index18.5 to 24.9Scale and height

Targets differ if you already have heart disease, diabetes or kidney disease. Your doctor will set those.

Blood pressure

High blood pressure rarely causes symptoms and is a leading cause of heart attack, stroke and kidney disease. Check it at least once a year. What your reading means.

Cholesterol

A lipid panel reports four values:

  • LDL ("bad"): lower is better. Under 100 mg/dL is optimal for most adults.
  • HDL ("good"): 40 mg/dL or more for men, 50 or more for women.
  • Triglycerides: under 150 mg/dL (1.7 mmol/L).
  • Total: under 200 mg/dL (5.2 mmol/L).

Doctors now look less at any single value and more at your overall 10-year risk, which also counts age, blood pressure, smoking and diabetes.

Blood sugar

A1C reflects average glucose over three months. Under 5.7% is normal, 5.7 to 6.4% is prediabetes and 6.5% or higher suggests diabetes. Prediabetes is common, usually silent, and often reversible with modest weight loss and activity. Convert an A1C.

Waist and weight

Your waist should be less than half your height. This catches risky abdominal fat that BMI misses. Use both: waist-to-height ratio and BMI.

Resting heart rate

Count your pulse for 60 seconds after sitting quietly for five minutes. Lower generally reflects better fitness; many active people sit in the 50s. A resting rate that is consistently over 100, or a rhythm that feels irregular, deserves a check.

Keep a record

Write your numbers down with the date, or use My numbers, which stores them in your browser. Bring the list to appointments. The know-your-numbers week walks you through collecting them one day at a time.

Questions people ask

How often should I check my numbers?

Blood pressure at least yearly; cholesterol every 4 to 6 years for low-risk adults and more often with risk factors; blood sugar every 3 years from 35 if overweight. Your doctor may advise differently.

Which number matters most?

For most adults, blood pressure. It is the largest modifiable risk factor for heart attack and stroke worldwide.
