Sitting less: how to break up a desk day

What long sitting does, how much movement offsets it, and practical ways to interrupt it.

By the Healthy.Life editorial team. 4 minute read. Last reviewed 19 November 2026.

The short version

  • Long unbroken sitting is linked with higher risk of heart disease and type 2 diabetes.
  • About 30 to 40 minutes of moderate activity a day appears to offset a day of sitting.
  • Stand up and move for 2 to 3 minutes every half hour.

What the evidence says

Adults in office jobs often sit for 9 to 10 hours a day. Large studies link high sitting time with greater risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes and early death. The risk is steepest in people who also do little exercise.

Two findings are encouraging:

  • Pooled data from people wearing activity monitors suggest 30 to 40 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity a day largely cancels the extra risk of sitting for 10 hours.
  • Short movement breaks help in their own right. Trials find that two to five minutes of light walking every 30 minutes lowers blood sugar after meals and blood pressure.

The WHO's advice is simple: limit sedentary time, and replace it with movement of any intensity.

A desk-day routine

WhenWhat
Every 30 minutesStand, stretch, walk to the far end of the room
Phone callsTake them standing or walking
Lunch10 to 15 minutes outside on foot
Mid-afternoonStairs: two flights up and down
End of dayWalk part of the way home

A timer or a smartwatch reminder does the remembering for you. Drinking more water builds in breaks naturally.

Standing desks

Standing burns only a little more energy than sitting, and standing still all day brings its own aches. Use a standing desk to change position often rather than to stand for hours. Movement is the goal, not standing.

At home

The evening sofa is where many sitting hours hide. Stand during adverts or between episodes, do the washing up by hand, stretch on the floor while you watch. None of it is exercise, and all of it counts.

For people who cannot stand easily

Seated movement helps: arm circles, seated marching, leg extensions, changing position, chair-based exercise routines. The principle is the same: break up stillness.

Questions people ask

Is sitting the new smoking?

No. The risks of smoking are far larger. Sitting is a real but modest risk that regular activity mostly offsets.

How often should I get up?

Every 30 minutes is a good rule. Even one or two minutes of walking makes a measurable difference to blood sugar.
