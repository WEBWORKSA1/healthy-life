Nourish

Build a balanced plate

A visual way to eat well without counting anything: half vegetables and fruit, a quarter protein, a quarter whole grains.

By the Healthy.Life editorial team. 5 minute read. Last reviewed 19 November 2026.

The short version

  • Fill half the plate with vegetables and fruit.
  • Split the other half between protein foods and whole grains or starchy vegetables.
  • Cook with unsaturated oils, drink water, and keep highly processed foods occasional.

The plate

Picture a normal dinner plate:

ShareWhat goes thereExamples
HalfVegetables and fruitSalad, greens, carrots, tomatoes, peppers, berries, an apple
A quarterProtein foodsBeans, lentils, fish, eggs, chicken, tofu, yoghurt, nuts
A quarterWhole grains or starchy vegetablesBrown rice, oats, wholemeal bread or pasta, potatoes with skin
On the sideHealthy fats and waterOlive or rapeseed oil, a few nuts; water, tea or coffee

It works for a bowl, a lunchbox or a takeaway too. Ask: where are the vegetables, where is the protein, and is the grain a whole one?

Why this pattern

Dietary patterns built this way, such as Mediterranean-style eating, are consistently linked with lower rates of heart disease, type 2 diabetes and some cancers. The World Health Organization's core advice matches it: at least 400 g (five portions) of vegetables and fruit a day, less salt, less free sugar, and unsaturated fats in place of saturated and trans fats.

The 2025–2030 U.S. Dietary Guidelines put more weight on protein and on avoiding highly processed foods, and less on limiting full-fat dairy. Nutrition scientists disagree about some of that emphasis. What nobody disputes: eat more vegetables, fruit, pulses and whole grains, and fewer sugary drinks and packaged snacks.

Portions without scales

  • Protein: the palm of your hand.
  • Grains or starch: a cupped hand.
  • Vegetables: two open handfuls.
  • Fats: the tip of your thumb.

Bigger or more active people need more; let hunger guide second helpings of vegetables and protein first.

Five changes that move the needle

  1. Add a vegetable to breakfast or lunch, where they are usually missing.
  2. Swap one refined grain for a whole one.
  3. Eat beans or lentils twice a week.
  4. Replace sugary drinks with water.
  5. Cook one more meal at home each week than you do now.

On a budget

Frozen and canned vegetables and fruit (in water or juice, not syrup) are as nutritious as fresh and cheaper. Dried or canned pulses, oats, eggs and tinned fish are some of the best-value foods in any shop.

Questions people ask

Do I have to cut out carbs?

No. The type matters more than the amount. Whole grains, pulses, fruit and vegetables are carbohydrates linked with better health.

Is fruit bad because of sugar?

No. Whole fruit comes with fibre and water, and eating it is linked with better health. Fruit juice is different; keep it to a small glass.
