BMI: what it tells you and what it misses

Where BMI came from, why doctors still use it, and three measures that fill in its blind spots.

By the Healthy.Life editorial team. 4 minute read. Last reviewed 19 November 2026.

The short version

  • BMI is a quick screen of weight for height, designed for populations.
  • It cannot see muscle, fat distribution, age or ethnicity.
  • Add waist-to-height ratio and your blood results for a real picture.

A 190-year-old formula

Body mass index is weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. A Belgian statistician devised it in the 1830s to describe populations. It was adopted for health screening in the 1970s because it is cheap, quick and tracks body fat reasonably well across large groups.

Why it is still used

At the population level, health risk rises as BMI moves above 25 and climbs steeply above 30: more type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease, sleep apnoea, osteoarthritis and several cancers. Risk also rises below 18.5. As a first-pass screen it does its job.

What it misses

Blind spotWhat happens
MuscleMuscular people are labelled overweight
Fat distributionAbdominal fat is riskier than fat on hips and thighs; BMI treats them alike
AgeOlder adults lose muscle, so a "healthy" BMI can hide high body fat
EthnicityRisk begins at lower BMIs in South Asian, Chinese, Black African and some other groups
SexWomen carry more fat than men at the same BMI

Studies find that a substantial share of people with a "normal" BMI have unhealthy blood pressure, blood sugar or cholesterol, and many people with a BMI over 25 have none of these.

Three better companions

  • Waist-to-height ratio. Keep your waist under half your height. UK guidance now recommends it alongside BMI. Work out yours.
  • Blood pressure, blood sugar and lipids. These measure the consequences that weight is meant to predict.
  • Fitness and strength. Being fit lowers risk at any size. Can you walk briskly for 30 minutes and get up from the floor without using your hands?

In 2025 an international commission proposed that obesity be diagnosed with BMI plus at least one body-size measure such as waist, and judged by whether excess fat is affecting organs or daily life. Many health systems are moving the same way.

How to use your BMI

Treat it as a prompt. Inside the healthy band with a waist under half your height: carry on. Outside it: take the other measurements before drawing conclusions, and talk to a doctor about the whole picture.

Weight is one input to health, not a measure of your worth or effort.

Tools for this

  • BMI calculatorYour body mass index, its category and your healthy weight range.
  • Waist-to-height ratioWhether your waist is under half your height, the simplest check of risky belly fat.
  • Body fat calculatorAn estimate of body fat percentage from a tape measure, using the U.S. Navy method.
  • Healthy weight rangeThe span of weights that counts as healthy for your height.

Questions people ask

Is BMI outdated?

Incomplete rather than useless. It remains a reasonable first screen, and is now increasingly paired with waist measures.

What is a healthy BMI for my ethnicity?

Some countries use lower cut-offs for Asian populations: overweight from 23 and obesity from 27.5. Ask your doctor which apply to you.
