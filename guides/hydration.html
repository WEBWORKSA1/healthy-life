Hydration: myths and facts

How much to drink, what counts, and how to tell if you are getting enough without measuring anything.

By the Healthy.Life editorial team. 4 minute read. Last reviewed 19 November 2026.

The short version

  • Most adults need about 2 to 3 litres of drinks a day, more with exercise and heat.
  • Tea, coffee and milk count. Food supplies about a fifth of your water.
  • Pale-yellow urine and little thirst mean you are doing fine.

How much

The U.S. National Academies set adequate intakes for total water of 3.7 litres a day for men and 2.7 for women, from all drinks and food. Drinks make up roughly 3.0 and 2.2 litres of that. The NHS gives a simpler message: 6 to 8 glasses a day.

These are averages for temperate conditions. You need more when you sweat, in hot weather, at altitude, with fever, vomiting or diarrhoea, and when pregnant or breastfeeding.

Myths

"Only water counts." Milk, tea, coffee, soup and juicy foods all hydrate. Water is the best default because it has no sugar or calories.

"Coffee dehydrates you." At normal intakes it does not. The water in the cup more than covers caffeine's mild diuretic effect.

"If you are thirsty, it is already too late." Thirst is an early, reliable signal in healthy adults. It is less dependable in older people, who should drink regularly through the day.

"More is always better." Drinking far beyond thirst has no benefit and, in extreme cases, dilutes blood sodium dangerously.

"Clear urine is the goal." Pale yellow is the target. Completely clear usually means more than you need.

Signs you need more

Dark-yellow urine, a dry mouth, headache, tiredness, dizziness and trouble concentrating. In older adults, confusion can be a sign.

During exercise

For sessions under an hour, drink to thirst. For longer or sweatier efforts, about 400 to 800 ml an hour is a common guide, with some salt if you are going for hours. Weighing yourself before and after shows how much you lose: each kilo is about a litre.

Making it easy

  • Drink a glass when you wake and one with each meal.
  • Keep a bottle where you can see it.
  • Flavour water with citrus, mint, cucumber or berries.
  • Eat water-rich foods: fruit, vegetables, soup, yoghurt.

When to be careful

People with heart failure or kidney disease, and anyone told to restrict fluids, should follow their clinician's limit.

Questions people ask

How do I know if I am dehydrated?

Check your urine colour. Dark yellow with thirst, headache or fatigue points to mild dehydration.

Is sparkling water as good as still?

Yes for hydration. Plain sparkling water is fine for teeth; flavoured versions with citric acid or sugar are less so.
