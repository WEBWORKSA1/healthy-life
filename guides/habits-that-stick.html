Habits that stick: what the research says

Why motivation fails, how long a habit takes to form, and a five-step method for making a change last.

By the Healthy.Life editorial team. 4 minute read. Last reviewed 19 November 2026.

The short version

  • Habits form by repeating one small action in the same context.
  • On average it takes about two months, and missing a day does not reset the clock.
  • Make it tiny, tie it to an existing routine and track it.

Why willpower runs out

Roughly four in ten of our daily actions are habits: things done with little thought in response to a cue. Motivation is what starts a change; habit is what keeps it going when motivation dips, which it always does.

How long it takes

In the best-known study, people repeated a chosen health behaviour daily. Automaticity levelled off after a median of 66 days, with a range of 18 to 254. Simple actions such as drinking a glass of water became automatic fastest; exercise took longest.

Two findings matter most:

  • Missing one day made no measurable difference. Give up the idea of a perfect streak.
  • Consistency of context mattered. Same time, same place, same trigger.

The five-step method

  1. Shrink it. Choose a version so small it feels almost silly: two minutes of stretching, one vegetable, a walk to the end of the road. You can do more; you only have to do the minimum.
  2. Anchor it. Attach it to something you already do every day: "After I put the kettle on, I will do ten squats."
  3. Make it easy. Lay out shoes the night before. Put fruit at eye level. Remove one step.
  4. Track it. Tick a calendar. Visible progress is rewarding in itself. Our challenges are built around this.
  5. Plan for misses. Decide now what you will do when you slip: "If I miss a day, I do the two-minute version tomorrow." Never miss twice.

Breaking a habit

It is easier to replace than to erase. Keep the cue and the reward; change the action. If the 3 pm slump sends you to the vending machine, the cue is the slump and the reward is a break. Walk for five minutes with a piece of fruit instead.

Adding friction helps too: keep snacks out of sight, log out of apps, leave your phone in another room.

One thing at a time

People who change one behaviour at a time are more likely to succeed than those who overhaul everything on 1 January. Pick the change that would make the others easier. For many people that is sleep.

Be on your own side

Self-criticism after a slip predicts giving up. Treating a lapse as information, not failure, predicts carrying on.

Questions people ask

Does it really take 21 days to form a habit?

No. That figure comes from a 1960 self-help book. Research puts the average closer to two months, with wide variation.

What is habit stacking?

Attaching a new behaviour to an established one, so the old habit becomes the cue for the new.
