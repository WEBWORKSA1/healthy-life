The numbers behind the range

WhoGrams per kg of body weight a day
Minimum to prevent deficiency (RDA)0.8
Adults, 2025–2030 U.S. Dietary Guidelines1.2 to 1.6
Building muscle with strength training1.6 to 2.2
Regular endurance training1.4 to 1.8
Aged 65 and over1.2 to 1.6, spread across meals

Example: at 70 kg, general health: 84 to 112 g a day, or roughly 28 to 37 g at each of three meals.

The 0.8 g figure is a floor, not a goal. The 2025–2030 U.S. guidelines raised the suggested range for adults; some nutrition scientists think that goes further than the evidence requires for people who are not training or older. Either way, amounts in these ranges are safe for healthy adults.

What that looks like in food

  • 100 g cooked chicken breast: about 31 g
  • 100 g firm tofu: about 17 g
  • 170 g plain Greek yoghurt: about 17 g
  • 1 large egg: about 6 g
  • 150 g cooked lentils: about 13 g
  • 100 g canned tuna: about 25 g

Plant and animal sources both count. Variety across the day covers all the amino acids you need.

Before you rely on this number

  • If you have kidney disease, do not raise protein without your doctor's advice.
  • If your BMI is over 30, base the calculation on a weight near the top of your healthy range, or the result will be too high.
  • More than about 2.2 g per kg brings no extra benefit for most people.

Questions people ask

How much protein do I need a day?

At least 0.8 g per kg of body weight. Most adults do well at 1.2 to 1.6 g per kg, more if you lift weights.

Is too much protein harmful?

Intakes up to about 2 g per kg are considered safe for healthy adults. People with kidney disease need individual limits.

Do I need protein powder?

No. It is a convenience. Ordinary foods cover the range for most people.
